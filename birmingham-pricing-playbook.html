Pricing is the strategy. Everything else is support.

Here's the real talk most agents won't put in writing: photography, staging, open houses, social media — all of it matters, and my team goes hard on all of it. But none of it can rescue a wrong price. Pricing is the one decision that touches every other outcome: how many buyers see your home, how fast they act, how strong their offers are, and how much you actually walk away with.

And here's the part sellers hate hearing but need to: you don't set your home's price. Neither do I. The market does. Our job — yours and mine — is to read the market correctly and position your home where it wins. That's what this playbook teaches you to do.

The price isn't a wish. It's a position on a battlefield. Pick the right ground and the buyers come to you.
STEP 1 — READ THE MARKET

The 5-5-5 Method

An algorithm didn't walk your street. A national website doesn't know your kitchen got redone or that the house two doors down backs up to a highway. When I price a home, I pull three sets of five comps — and each set answers a different question:

5
ACTIVE listings
Your competition. These are the homes a buyer will tour the same weekend they tour yours. If they out-shine you at the same price, you lose the showing.
5
PENDING sales
What's winning right now. Pendings are the freshest signal in the market — the prices that just convinced a real buyer to sign. This is where the market is headed.
5
SOLD comps
What actually closed — and what an appraiser will see. Sold prices are where your buyer's lender lives. Price too far above them and the deal can crack at appraisal even after you get an offer.

How to read comps like a strategist

  • Adjust, don't average. A comp with a renovated kitchen and yours without isn't a match — it's a math problem. Condition, updates, lot, garage, school zone: each one moves the number.
  • Weight the recent and the close. A sale from last month on your street beats a sale from last year across town. Every time.
  • Watch the actives that aren't selling. A house sitting unsold at $400K isn't evidence your home is worth $400K — it's evidence the market already said no at $400K. Sitting inventory is a warning, not a comp.
  • Pendings break ties. When actives and solds disagree, the pendings tell you which direction the market is moving.

Fifteen data points, three angles, one defensible answer. That's how the market tells you the price — you just have to be willing to listen.

STEP 2 — POSITION THE PRICE

The band beats the anchor

Almost every seller walks in with an anchor number — what a neighbor got, what the online estimate says, what they need for the next house, what the house "should" be worth after everything they put into it. I get it. But the anchor is about you. The band is about the market.

The 5-5-5 work produces a range — a band where the data says your home sells. The strategy question isn't "what's the biggest number I can put on it?" It's "where inside the band do I position to create the most competition for my home?"

Why position inside the band

  • Search brackets are real. Buyers shop in ranges — up to $350K, $350–400K. A price just above a bracket line hides your home from the exact buyers most likely to stretch for it. A price positioned at or just under the line puts you in two searches instead of one.
  • Strong positioning creates competition. Price at the market and multiple buyers show up; multiple buyers is how homes sell at or over asking. Price above it and you get silence — and silence never negotiates up.
  • The appraisal has a vote. Even if one buyer bites at an above-band price, the lender's appraiser is reading the same solds you just read. The band protects your contract, not just your showing count.
A true story from my own files (no names, no address): a seller listed with a discount flat-fee service at an anchor price of $355,000. Cut to $345,000. Still sat. The home didn't sell — it sat vacant for over a year. When I ran the actual comps, the data supported a band in the low $320s. The anchor felt like protecting her money. It cost her a year of carrying costs and a stale listing — and the market never once asked how she felt about it. We relisted it the right way: priced inside the band, marketed like it mattered.
STEP 3 — RESPECT THE WINDOW

The first 14 days decide the fight

The day your home hits the market, something happens that will never happen again: every serious buyer waiting for a home like yours gets notified at once. Saved searches fire. Agents send it to clients. The buyers who've been losing out on other homes — the motivated ones, the pre-approved ones — see it in the first hours.

That's your window. Those first two weeks are when your home has maximum attention and zero baggage. It's when competition happens, and competition — not negotiation tricks — is what pushes a price up.

What the window means for strategy

  • Launch ready, not "we'll fix it as we go." Photos, prep, and price all right on day one — because day one is the biggest audience you will ever have. (On my listings, the full marketing launch goes live within 72 hours of go.)
  • The "test price" is a trap. "Let's start high and see what happens" spends your best two weeks on a price the data doesn't support. The buyers who would have competed for it see it, pass, and move on. When you cut later, you're marketing to a smaller, more skeptical audience.
  • Days on market becomes the story. Once a listing sits, buyers stop asking "what's it worth?" and start asking "what's wrong with it?" — and every offer that follows is priced with that discount built in.
You don't get a second first week. Price for the window, and the window does the negotiating for you.

THE ALGORITHM QUESTION

Why the online estimate isn't a price

I'm not going to mock the Zestimate — it's a starting point, and it's free. But understand what it is: a statistical guess made by a model that has never been inside your home, your street's homes, or any home. It can't see condition, updates, layout, light, smell, or the fact that your backyard is the best on the block.

Here's what tells you everything: look at the range, not the number. On one home in my own files, the estimate's own published range spanned $43,000 — the model itself admitting it could be off by the price of a new car in either direction. A range that wide isn't a price. It's a shrug.

Algorithms don't price homes. Strategists do — with fifteen comps, a walk-through, and skin in the game. Use the estimate as one input. Never let it be the anchor.

TIMING

List now or wait? The honest checklist

I don't do slogans about timing, and I won't pressure a maybe. Sometimes waiting is the right call — but only when it's a strategy, not a stall. Run yourself through this honestly:

Listing now is probably right if…

  • Your next move is clear — you know where you're going and the math works at today's numbers, not hoped-for ones.
  • The home can be launch-ready: prepped, photographed, priced in the band, live inside the window.
  • Your motivation is real. Committed sellers make clean decisions; "let's just test it" sellers make expensive ones.
  • Waiting has a real cost you're currently ignoring — carrying two homes, a vacant property, a relocation clock, an estate to settle.

Waiting is probably right if…

  • There's a specific, dated reason — a renovation that genuinely moves the band, a lease ending, a job decision landing next quarter.
  • You'd refuse an in-band offer if it came tomorrow. If the band's answer is a no, don't list — a listing you won't sell just burns your home's fresh-debut card.
  • You can name what has to be true for waiting to win — and it's something evidence could actually deliver, not a hope that the market owes you a bigger number.

That last one is the strategist's test: "I'll wait" is only a plan if you can finish the sentence "…because by then, ___ will be true." If you can't, waiting is just an anchor with a calendar.

QUICK REFERENCE

The five pricing mistakes I see most

  • Pricing the memory. What you paid, what you spent, what the neighbor got in a different market — none of it is a comp.
  • Padding "room to negotiate." The pad doesn't create negotiation. It prevents the showings where negotiation would have started.
  • Testing high "for a couple weeks." Those are the only two weeks the market hands you for free — the most expensive test you can run.
  • Treating the estimate as an appraisal. One input, wide range, zero walk-throughs. Respect it as a guess; never obey it as a price.
  • Cutting slow and small. If the market says you're high, one decisive move back into the band beats three timid cuts that teach buyers to wait you out.
